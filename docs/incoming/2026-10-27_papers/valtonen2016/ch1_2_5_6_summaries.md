# Chapters 1, 2, 5 and 6: three-body facts only

Valtonen et al. 2016. Short summaries, listing only the three-body facts these chapters contain. Page mapping:
Chapters 1–2 book pp. 1–49, **PDF = book + 12** (book p. 50, a blank verso, is absent from the PDF); Chapters 5–6
book pp. 99–130, PDF = book + 11. Our words throughout.

## Chapter 1, "Classical Problems" (pp. 1–30)

A history from eclipses to Kepler, with the two-body problem. Three-body facts:

- The problem in its modern form is Newton's (*Principia*, 1687): relative motion of three point masses under
  universal gravitation (p. 4).
- **Euler (1763)**, Berlin: three bodies can always lie on a rotating straight line (published in *Novi Commentarii*,
  St Petersburg). **Lagrange (1772)**, Paris: three bodies can always sit at the vertices of a rotating equilateral
  triangle. Both exist for any masses (p. 4). Fig. 1.2 (p. 5) draws the equal-mass cases and calls them "stable triple
  systems"; that word is the book's (the equal-mass collinear and Lagrange configurations are linearly unstable in
  the standard literature; nothing in the repo addresses it, so it is noted, not flagged as a conflict).
- Trojans and Greeks at Jupiter's L4 and L5, **60°** ahead and behind (Fig. 1.3, p. 5).
- **Figure-eight**: Moore, **1993**, by computer; proved by Chenciner and Montgomery; periodic (p. 6, Fig. 1.4).
  JA and VO "came close to the discovery … a decade before Moore" (p. 6, footnote 7).
- **Sundman (1912)** series; **Belorizky (1930)**: **10⁸⁰,⁰⁰⁰** terms to solve "Lagrange's triangular three body
  problem with the usual accuracy of astronomical observations, and just for one sixth of the period in time" (p. 6).
  Chapter 7 (p. 136) gives the same count for one orbital period of Sun–Jupiter–Saturn and dates Sundman 1913.
- OJ287 as a three-body problem (two black holes and a gas cloud), light travel time **3.45 billion years**, flare of
  **13 September 2007** (p. 7); no-hair test (p. 8).
- The idealisations: point masses; no other bodies; bodies exist forever unless they collide; exact Newtonian
  gravity (pp. 8–9). Varying an orbit in every independent way means choosing from **an 11-dimensional space**
  (p. 9). (The book does not say how 11 is counted.)
- Two-body background: escape velocities **11 km/s** (Earth), **2 km/s** (Moon), **618 km/s** (Sun), and **42 km/s**
  from the Sun at the Earth's orbit (p. 25); conic sections and eccentricity (pp. 18–19, 27).

## Chapter 2, "From Newton to Einstein" (pp. 31–49)

Newton's laws, least action, relativity. Three-body facts:

- The Sun's gravitational pull on the Moon is "more than double" the Earth's (p. 31, footnote 1).
- The Earth–Venus–Sun problem as Newton's worry about Solar System stability (p. 32).
- Nothing else specific to three bodies.

## Chapter 5, "The Solar System" (pp. 99–116)

- **Stability** (pp. 104–105): Laplace's averaging argument (two planets and the Sun); Laskar's integration over
  **more than 200 million years**, with a **15 m** uncertainty in the Earth's present position making its orbital phase
  unpredictable tens of millions of years ahead; **Ito & Tanikawa (2002)**, nine planets for longer than the age of the
  Solar System; **Laskar & Gastineau (2009)**, with relativity, longer than **5 billion years**, **2500** futures: in
  **1 %** Mercury's eccentricity grows until it crosses Venus's orbit; in one case the Earth collides about
  **3.3 billion years** ahead; in a closer study of Mars–Earth encounters, nearly **200** collisions, **48** involving
  the Earth, and **5** ejections of Mars (pp. 104–105).
- **Climate cycles** (pp. 105–106): computable geological time scale back **50 million years** before chaos sets in.
- **Lagrangian points** (pp. 106–108): L2 and L1 **1.5 million km** from the Earth; L3 behind the Sun; L4 and L5 at
  **60°**. The text says "The stability of this point [L1] was also discovered by Euler" (p. 107); L1 is the
  collinear (unstable) point, so "stability" is presumably the book's word for existence. 2010 TK7 at Earth's L4,
  about **300 m**, on a chaotic orbit (p. 107). The circular restricted three-body problem and Jacobi (p. 108).
- **Capture and collisions** (pp. 110–112): MV and Innanen (**1982**): capture of interstellar comets is much less
  likely than Hoyle thought; Zheng and MV: at most one Halley-sized interstellar comet reaching the Earth with a
  travel time up to **200 million years**; Öpik's (**1951**) statistical theory of two-body collisions in the
  three-body problem.
- **Triton** (pp. 113–114): binary–single encounters often split the binary, "The smallest body escapes while the
  middle size body becomes a satellite of the large body" (p. 113), a possible capture route.
- **Hill sphere** (p. 114): Hill's single radius; **Innanen (1979)** splits it by sense of motion: **70 %** of the
  Hill radius for prograde satellites, **144 %** for retrograde (Innanen 1979, AJ 84, 960, doi:10.1086/112499,
  Crossref; not in the repo). Cruithne, a quasi-satellite: last nudge from the Earth in **1902**, next in **2292**.
- Gravity assists, Rosetta (p. 115).

## Chapter 6, "Interacting Galaxies" (pp. 117–130)

- Star clusters: binaries and triples form even if absent initially; **Heggie (1975)** laid the foundations; single–
  binary encounters either harden the binary (single may leave the cluster, binary recoils) or form a bound triple
  that later breaks up statistically (p. 122).
- Holmberg (**1941**), light-bulb analogue, "Just over 72 particles" for two galaxies (p. 122). **Toomre & Toomre
  (1972)**: galaxy encounters as superposed three-body problems (two galaxy centres plus one star), a disk of **120**
  stars (pp. 122–123); Fig. 6.6's companion is **one third** of the main galaxy's mass (Toomre & Toomre 1972, ApJ 178,
  623, doi:10.1086/151823). Dark haloes about **ten times** the visible galaxy in extent and mass (p. 123).
- Bridges and tails as tidal three-body effects (p. 125); "soft ball" galaxy triples with Chandrasekhar's dynamical
  friction compared with Karachentsev's triple-galaxy catalogue (**1989**) and binary catalogue (**1987**); Zheng et al.
  start from groups of **five** galaxies (pp. 126–128).
- Local Group: Kahn & Woltjer (**1959**) timing mass, now **4000 billion M☉** against about **400 billion** stars
  (p. 129); small galaxies ejected by three-body processes collect near the zero-gravity surface about **5 million
  light years** out (Järnefelt **1933**) (pp. 129–130); Fig. 6.9 from Garrison-Kimmel et al. 2014, MNRAS 438, 2578,
  doi:10.1093/mnras/stt2377.
